Can We Trust AI-Generated Information?
                              --- By Ayush Singh Rathor, B.Tech CSE (AI & ML), VIT

[IMAGE — Header banner. Search: "AI trust digital verification concept."]

> "Fluent doesn't mean factual."

In my last blog, I talked about AI hallucination — the strange habit AI models have of stating false information with total confidence. This time, I want to take that idea out of theory and into the real world, because "AI can be wrong" sounds abstract until you see what actually happens when people trust it without checking.

So instead of starting with a definition, let me start with a story.

In 2023, a New York lawyer named Steven Schwartz used ChatGPT to research legal precedent for a personal injury case, Mata v. Avianca. The tool gave him case citations that looked completely real — case names, court details, legal reasoning, all of it. He even asked ChatGPT directly whether the cases were real, and the chatbot assured him they were.

[IMAGE — Search: "law court gavel document."]

They weren't. When the opposing lawyers tried to look up the cases, none of them existed. The federal judge overseeing the case found that the lawyers had submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations generated by ChatGPT, and the lawyers were fined for it. What makes this worth knowing isn't that a mistake happened once — it's that this wasn't a one-off case at all. Around 900 AI hallucinations have been documented in US court filings since 2023, and in 2026 a federal judge fined two lawyers a combined 110,000 dollars for submitting fabricated citations and invented quotations in a single case, the largest AI hallucination penalty in legal history. A confident-sounding citation is clearly not the same as a real one, and in professional fields, that gap can get extremely expensive.

Around the same time, something equally strange was happening at a much bigger scale. In May 2024, Google rolled out "AI Overviews," a feature that summarises search results directly at the top of the page using AI. Within days, screenshots went viral showing the tool giving genuinely bizarre advice, including telling people that eating rocks is good for their health and suggesting glue as a way to help cheese stick to pizza.

[IMAGE — Search: "search engine error concept."]

The rock advice traced back to a satirical article, and the glue suggestion came from an old Reddit post recommending nontoxic glue be added to pizza sauce. The AI didn't understand these were jokes. It just picked up the pattern of confident, matter-of-fact language and repeated it as if it were genuine advice. Google's own head of search later admitted the results highlighted specific areas the tool needed to improve, particularly how it handles nonsensical queries and satirical content. Which tells you something important: an AI model doesn't know the difference between a joke and a fact. It only recognises patterns in how confident language is written.

And it isn't only chatbots and search engines. In May 2025, the Chicago Sun-Times and The Philadelphia Inquirer published a summer reading list recommending books by well-known, real authors. Only five out of the fifteen recommended books were actually real. The rest were AI-generated inventions, complete with believable plot summaries, attributed to authors who had never written them.

[IMAGE — Search: "newspaper printing press books."]

The freelance writer responsible later admitted he had used AI to generate the list and, for some reason, failed to check or verify the material before submitting it. The newspaper called the situation unacceptable and launched a review into how it made it into print. This one stings a little more than the others, because unlike a courtroom filing or a search result, this was a printed newspaper — a source many readers still assume has been through a human editorial process before publication. AI-generated content can look polished and professionally written while being entirely fabricated, and apparently, even publishers can miss it.

A lawyer, a search engine, and a newspaper. Three completely different fields, but the same root cause every time: someone trusted AI-generated content the way they'd trust a verified source, without treating it as something that still needed checking. None of these were cases of AI "lying" on purpose either — AI doesn't have intentions. Each one was simply the model generating fluent, confident-sounding content that fit the shape of a correct answer, without that content actually being true. And the people involved weren't careless in some obvious, avoidable way — an experienced lawyer, a major tech company, and an established newspaper all fell into exactly the same trap. The more official something looks — a citation, a summary, a printed list — the more we tend to trust it without question, and AI happens to be very good at looking official.

[IMAGE — Search: "pattern connection concept illustration."]

So, can we trust AI-generated information? Honestly, the answer is: partially, and only with effort on our part. AI is genuinely useful for drafting, summarising, brainstorming, and getting a first pass at a topic. What it isn't good at is being the final word on facts, especially specific ones — names, numbers, quotes, citations, sources. Every story above could have been avoided with the same basic habit: treating an AI-generated fact as unverified until it's checked against an independent source, even if the AI itself insists it's correct. Confident tone is not evidence. AI has no way to sound unsure, even when it should be.

[IMAGE — Search: "checklist verification steps concept."]

> "The cost of trusting a wrong answer is always higher than the cost of checking it."

None of this is a reason to stop using AI — all three organisations involved in these stories still use AI tools today, and so will most of us. It's just a reminder that AI-generated information sits in a different category from verified information, even when it reads exactly the same way. The technology isn't going to suddenly develop hesitation in its voice. That responsibility stays with us — the people reading, publishing, and citing what it produces.
